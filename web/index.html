How it works

No turns

The board is continuous and both players play at once. A piece is a circle, its position is its centre, and it moves in a straight line to wherever you send it. There is no board of squares underneath: the squares are only a drawing.

Mana

Queuing a move costs mana, which refills continuously up to a cap. The cost is a flat amount plus a rate for the distance, taken the moment you queue the move and never refunded. Mana is the only limit on how often you can move.

A move, in three phases

  • Preparation. The piece stands still for a moment, showing that something is coming.
  • Movement. It travels at a fixed speed. This is the only phase in which it counts as moving.
  • Cooldown. It rests where it stopped and cannot be given a new move.

A piece captured during its preparation loses the move and the mana.

Where pieces may go

Rook, bishop and queen keep their directions and have no range limit. King and pawn keep their directions and reach one square, or one diagonal. Every direction is a narrow cone rather than a line, so a move can be aimed a few degrees off true. The knight has eight landing circles instead of eight squares.

Capture, in general

The general rule holds for every piece except the knight, and for the pawn only when it moves diagonally. The forward pawn and the knight are the exceptions, and the exceptions matter, so they have their own sections below.

  • A moving piece captures an enemy the instant their hitboxes touch, then carries on in the same direction and stops level with where that piece stood, or at its destination if that comes first.
  • If both pieces were moving when they touched, both are removed. A pawn pushing forward is the exception: it captures nothing, so it dies alone, and two forward pawns meeting head-on just stop against each other.
  • One enemy per move. After that the piece is spent: the next thing it touches blocks it instead, and it stops there.
  • Your own pieces are never captured and never passed through. A move that would run into one stops at the point of contact, having done nothing else.
  • A piece touched while it is still preparing is captured normally, and loses both the queued move and the mana.

Pawns

A pawn moving forward cannot capture at all. It stops the moment it touches any piece, enemy or friendly, and it takes nothing with it either way: run one into a moving enemy and you lose the pawn on its own. It can still be captured by an enemy that moves into it. From its starting square it may go two squares instead of one.

A pawn captures only by moving diagonally forward, and that move is legal only if an enemy is already sitting on the landing point when you queue it. The move itself is an ordinary one: the pawn takes the first enemy it touches on the way, exactly as a bishop would, so a piece that steps into the path is what dies and the piece you aimed at survives.

En passant. A pawn making a double move drops a marker at the point it passed one square from where it started, and it drops it while still travelling. The marker counts as an enemy piece for the diagonal capture, so you take it with an ordinary diagonal capture aimed at it, and taking it removes the pawn that left it, wherever that pawn has got to by then.

Only an enemy pawn moving diagonally can touch a marker. Nothing else sees it: it blocks no one, and a knight landing on it does not remove it. Taking one spends the pawn's one capture like any other, so a pawn that has already taken a piece this move cannot take a marker - it passes straight through and may come to rest on top of one, leaving it and its pawn alive.

You must answer with your very next queued move, though you may queue it while the double move is still running. Any other move queued once that pawn has finished lets the marker go.

A pawn promotes to a queen when its centre reaches the last rank. The move it is executing carries on unchanged: same direction, and it keeps the terms it started under. A forward push still captures nothing after becoming a queen, and a capture already spent is not handed back.

Knights

The knight breaks the general rules above, in its favour and against it.

  • In flight it can neither capture nor be captured, and nothing blocks it. It always completes its jump.
  • On arrival it removes every piece its hitbox overlaps, however many, friendly ones included. Landing on your own king loses you the game.
  • If any piece it removes was moving at that moment, the knight is removed too. The landing counts as part of its jump, so this is the ordinary both-were-moving rule. It costs the knight nothing else: it clears its landing point in full first and only then goes down with the moving piece, so a knight can take four pieces and still die.
  • Markers left by double-moving pawns are not pieces, and a knight landing on one does not remove it.

Castling

Queue a king move of more than one and up to two squares sideways. King and rook set off together and arrive together, overlapping on the way.

Winning

Take the enemy king. There is no check and no checkmate, so a king may walk anywhere, including somewhere it can be taken. Two kings colliding is a draw.

Account

An account gives you a rating and finds your games from any browser. Every game is recorded either way. Playing without an account works exactly as before, and always will.

Please use a password you use nowhere else. This is a hobby project. Passwords are hashed with argon2id and no email address is collected, but the security here has not been reviewed by anyone and comes with no guarantee. There is also no password reset: if you lose it, the account is gone.
